## Introduction

For more than a century, Australian law treated the continent as if no one had owned it before the British Crown. In 1992, *Mabo v Queensland [No 2]* rejected that premise and held that the common law recognises the rights of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples to their traditional lands.[^1] The decision is rightly regarded as a turning point. But the same judgments that recognised native title also made it vulnerable, by holding that it may be extinguished by any valid exercise of sovereign power inconsistent with its continued enjoyment.

This essay argues that the law since *Mabo* has given native title a secure place in Australian law only on terms that leave it fragile: recognition depends on proof of continuity that dispossession itself makes hard to establish, and extinguishment turns on the rights others were granted rather than on the rights of the traditional owners. Part II traces how native title came to be recognised, first by the common law and then by statute. Part III examines extinguishment and the compensation now payable for it. Part IV considers the strongest objection to this argument: that the scheme buys certainty for all landholders at a price the law has since tempered. Part V concludes.

## Recognition

### From Terra Nullius to *Mabo*

The starting point was a characterisation of the colony rather than a finding of fact. In *Cooper v Stuart*, the Privy Council described New South Wales as 'a tract of territory practically unoccupied, without settled inhabitants or settled law'.[^2] On that view, English law arrived with the settlers, and there was no prior system of land law for it to recognise. The first attempt to establish native title in an Australian court, *Milirrpum v Nabalco Pty Ltd*, failed for that reason, even though Blackburn J accepted that the Yolngu plaintiffs had 'a subtle and elaborate system' of law.[^3] A later claim that Aboriginal people retained a form of sovereignty was also rejected.[^4]

Two developments prepared the ground for change. The first was legislation. When Queensland attempted to extinguish any traditional rights in the Murray Islands without compensation, the High Court held the attempt inoperative because it denied the Meriam people the equality before the law guaranteed by s 10 of the *Racial Discrimination Act 1975* (Cth).[^5] The second was international. Australia's accession to the First Optional Protocol to the *International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights* exposed the common law to international standards of non-discrimination, including the rights of minorities to enjoy their own culture.[^6]

Against that background, the majority in *Mabo* rejected the extended notion of terra nullius. Brennan J, with whom Mason CJ and McHugh J agreed, explained why the common law could no longer rest on it:

> Whatever the justification advanced in earlier days for refusing to recognize the rights and interests in land of the indigenous inhabitants of settled colonies, an unjust and discriminatory doctrine of that kind can no longer be accepted. The expectations of the international community accord in this respect with the contemporary values of the Australian people.[^7]

The common law, Brennan J said, should not be 'frozen in an age of racial discrimination'.[^8]

### The Statutory Definition

Parliament responded with the *Native Title Act 1993* (Cth),[^9] which the Prime Minister described in his second reading speech as a response to the High Court's decision.[^10] The Act now defines native title. Section 223(1) requires rights and interests possessed under the traditional laws acknowledged and customs observed by the people concerned, by which they have a connection with the land or waters, and which are recognised by the common law.[^11] The definition draws directly on Brennan J's account of native title as having its origin in, and being given its content by, traditional laws and customs.[^12]

The requirement of continuity has proved the hardest to meet. In *Members of the Yorta Yorta Aboriginal Community v Victoria*, the High Court held that the laws and customs must be those of a society that has continued to acknowledge and observe them, substantially uninterrupted, since the Crown acquired sovereignty.[^13] The claim failed. That result gives effect to Brennan J's warning that when 'the tide of history' has washed away any real acknowledgment of traditional law, 'the foundation of native title has disappeared'.[^14] The difficulty is that the tide was not neutral: the people least able to prove continuity are often those most thoroughly dispossessed. The Australian Law Reform Commission has recommended amending s 223 to make clear, among other things, that traditional laws and customs may adapt and evolve.[^15]

## Extinguishment

### Inconsistency of Rights

#### Pastoral Leases

Extinguishment turns on inconsistency between native title and the rights granted to others. In *Wik Peoples v Queensland*, a majority of the High Court held that the pastoral leases in question did not confer a right of exclusive possession, and so did not necessarily extinguish native title.[^16] The two could coexist; where they conflicted, the rights of the pastoralist would prevail.[^17] The decision was welcomed by Indigenous parties and resisted by pastoral and mining interests, and Parliament responded with the *Native Title Amendment Act 1998* (Cth), which confirmed the extinguishing effect of many past grants.[^18]

#### Freehold and Partial Extinguishment

A grant of freehold, by contrast, extinguishes native title completely, and it does not revive if the land later returns to the Crown.[^19] In *Western Australia v Ward*, the High Court described native title as a bundle of rights and interests, any of which may be extinguished separately by an inconsistent grant.[^20] The test asks what rights the grant conferred, not how the land was in fact used.[^21] The result is a law of 'partial extinguishment' in which the rights of the traditional owners are measured, piece by piece, against the rights of others. Much of the commentary since *Mabo* has criticised this approach as compromising the recognition the decision promised.[^22]

### Compensation

Where native title is extinguished or impaired, the *Native Title Act* entitles the native title holders to compensation on just terms.[^23] The High Court first assessed that compensation in *Northern Territory v Griffiths*, which concerned the town of Timber Creek.[^24] The Court valued the economic loss by reference to the freehold value of the land, reduced to reflect the nature of the rights lost, and awarded $1.3 million for the cultural and spiritual loss suffered by the Ngaliwurru and Nungali peoples.[^25] *Griffiths* does not restore extinguished rights, but it gives the law of extinguishment a price, and in doing so recognises the loss as a loss of property and of connection.[^26]

## Counter-arguments: Certainty and Survival

It may be argued that the fragility of native title is the price of certainty. A doctrine that allowed native title to defeat existing grants would have unsettled the titles of farmers, miners and homeowners across the continent, and the *Native Title Act* was designed to validate past grants and give future dealings a clear procedure.[^27] On this view, the law did what it could: it recognised native title where it survived, and provided compensation where it did not.

That argument has force, and the law has moved some way towards it. In *Akiba v Commonwealth*, the High Court held that legislation regulating commercial fishing did not extinguish the Torres Strait Islanders' native title right to take resources for commercial purposes, because regulating a right is not inconsistent with its existence.[^28] *Griffiths* has made extinguishment costly. And the High Court has since recognised the distinctive connection of Aboriginal Australians with the land in holding that they are not 'aliens' within the meaning of the *Constitution*.[^29] But certainty was bought at the expense of one group of landholders only. The Commonwealth's power to make laws with respect to the people of any race was used, in the Hindmarsh Island bridge litigation, to enact a law that operated to the detriment of the traditional owners concerned.[^30] Certainty for some, in short, has meant precariousness for others.

## Conclusion

*Mabo* changed the foundations of Australian land law, and it did so by correcting a doctrine its own judgments described as unjust and discriminatory.[^31] Yet the law that has grown from it makes recognition depend on continuity that dispossession disrupted, and measures what survives against the rights granted to others. Compensation and the narrowing of extinguishment have softened that result without changing it. Whether the reasoning in *Love v Commonwealth* will lead the law to see the connection of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples to their country in a different way remains open.
